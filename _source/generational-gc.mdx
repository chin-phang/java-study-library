## 1. The problem it exists to solve

Two services. Service A allocates 2 GB per second of short-lived objects.
Service B allocates 50 MB per second. Service B spends four times as much time
in GC and has worse pause times.

Everyone's instinct — "allocation is expensive, reduce allocation" — predicts
the opposite. The instinct is wrong, and the reason it's wrong is the single
most useful thing to understand about the JVM's collectors.

Service B holds onto its objects. Service A doesn't.

**GC cost scales with the volume of data that survives, not with the volume
allocated.** Garbage is nearly free. Survivors are expensive. Once you have that
inverted correctly, most GC advice you'll read sorts itself into useful and
useless.

## 2. Allocation is a pointer bump

Start with why allocating is cheap, because it's not obvious coming from C.

`malloc` has to find a suitably sized free block in a fragmented heap — search a
free list, split a block, update bookkeeping. The JVM does none of that,
because a copying collector leaves Eden **contiguous and empty** after every
collection. So allocation is:

```
if (top + size > end) slowPath();
result = top;
top += size;
```

A bounds check and an add. Roughly ten instructions, and the JIT inlines it into
your code — there is no call.

Better still, each thread gets its own **TLAB** (Thread-Local Allocation
Buffer), a private slab carved out of Eden. So `top` is thread-local and the
bump needs no atomic operation and no lock. Threads only synchronise when a TLAB
is exhausted and a new one is requested.

The consequence: allocating a small object in Java is *faster than `malloc`*,
and the "object pooling is faster" instinct inherited from C++ is usually wrong
on the JVM. Pooling replaces a pointer bump with a data structure, and it
promotes objects into the old generation where they cost you at every collection
(section 4).

## 3. The weak generational hypothesis

The empirical observation the whole design rests on:

> **Most objects die young.** The longer an object has lived, the longer it is
> likely to continue living.

Measured across real workloads, typically 90–98% of objects are unreachable by
the next collection. A request handler allocates parameter objects, strings,
intermediate collections, a response — and all of it is garbage the moment the
response is written.

That suggests a specific strategy: **don't trace the whole heap to find a few
survivors.** Instead, split the heap and collect the young part frequently.

### Why a copying collector makes garbage free

A young collection uses copying (Cheney-style): trace from the roots, and copy
every *live* object out of Eden into a survivor space. When that's done,
everything remaining in Eden is garbage — and you reclaim it by **doing
nothing**. Just reset the allocation pointer to the start.

Read that again, because it's the crux: dead objects are never visited, never
touched, never individually freed. The collector's work is proportional to the
number of live objects, and completely independent of how much garbage there is.

```
minor GC cost ≈ f(live objects in Eden)
              ≈ 0 when everything died
```

You also get compaction for free — survivors are copied into a contiguous
block — which is why Java heaps don't fragment the way `malloc` heaps do.

So Service A, allocating 2 GB/s of objects that all die, does frequent
collections that each find almost nothing to copy. Cheap. Service B allocates
less but promotes steadily, so every collection copies real data and the old
generation keeps growing toward a much more expensive collection.

## 4. Ageing, promotion, and how it goes wrong

Objects don't go straight to the old generation. The layout is Eden plus two
survivor spaces, S0 and S1, one always empty:

1. Allocate in Eden.
2. Minor GC: copy live objects from Eden **and** the occupied survivor space
   into the empty survivor space. Increment each object's age.
3. Swap the survivor roles. Eden is now empty.
4. When an object's age passes the **tenuring threshold**, promote it to the old
   generation.

Age is stored in a few bits of the object header's mark word — which is why the
maximum tenuring threshold is 15.

Two failure modes matter:

**Premature promotion.** If the survivor space is too small to hold the
survivors of one collection, objects are promoted immediately regardless of age.
Now short-lived objects are in the old generation, where only a major
collection can reclaim them. The old gen fills, and you pay for a big
collection to clean up things that would have died in Eden for free. This is a
very common misconfiguration and it presents as "old gen keeps growing" without
a leak.

**Allocation rate outrunning the collector.** With G1, if evacuation can't find
free regions for survivors, you get *evacuation failure* — expensive in-place
handling, and repeated failures degrade to a full GC
([Q74](/docs/java/jvm-memory-gc#q74)).

### The write barrier and remembered sets

There's a problem with collecting the young generation independently: an old
object may hold a reference to a young one, and that reference is a root you
must not miss. Tracing the old generation to find them would defeat the purpose.

So the JVM maintains a **card table**: the heap is divided into cards (typically
512 bytes), and every reference-field write executes a tiny **write barrier**
that marks the card dirty. At collection time, only dirty cards are scanned for
old→young references.

This is why every `obj.field = other` in Java carries a small hidden cost, and
why G1's **remembered sets** — per-region records of incoming references — can
consume a meaningful fraction of the heap on large, reference-dense workloads.
It's also the mechanism behind generational ZGC's improvement in Java 21: adding
generations let ZGC stop scanning everything on every cycle.

## 5. What this means in practice

**"Reduce allocation" is usually the wrong advice.** The right question is
"what is surviving that shouldn't?" A cache with no eviction, a growing static
collection, a `ThreadLocal` on a pooled thread, an oversized batch held across
a transaction — those cost you. A million short-lived `Integer` boxes usually do
not.

**Two numbers tell you almost everything**
([Q77](/docs/java/jvm-memory-gc#q77)):

| Number | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Allocation rate (MB/s) | How fast Eden fills → how *often* you collect |
| Promotion rate (MB/s) | How much survives → how *expensive* each collection is, and how fast old gen fills |

High allocation with low promotion is healthy. Low allocation with steady
promotion is the shape of a problem.

**And the JIT may delete the allocation entirely.** If escape analysis proves an
object can't be observed outside the compiled unit, C2 performs scalar
replacement — the object's fields become registers and no allocation happens at
all ([Q80](/docs/java/jvm-memory-gc#q80)). This is fragile (it depends on
inlining succeeding), but it's another reason micro-optimising allocation by
hand often achieves nothing measurable.

## 6. What this does *not* cover

- **Old generation collection.** Different algorithms entirely — G1's concurrent
  mark plus mixed evacuation, ZGC's concurrent relocation with load barriers.
  Generational theory tells you how to *avoid* needing them, not how they work.
- **Humongous objects.** In G1, anything over half a region bypasses Eden
  entirely and is allocated in contiguous old-gen regions. A workload allocating
  8 MB byte arrays with a 4 MB region size never benefits from any of this.
- **Non-generational collectors.** Pre-21 ZGC and Shenandoah were
  single-generation; the hypothesis still held, they just couldn't exploit it.
- **Pause time.** Generational design reduces GC *work*. Pause length also
  depends on safepoint arrival ([Q78](/docs/java/jvm-memory-gc#q78)), which is
  a separate mechanism and a common source of "the GC log says 10ms but the
  request took 500ms".

## 7. Lab: prove garbage is free

### Lab 1 — allocate enormously, pay almost nothing

```java
public class Churn {
    public static void main(String[] a) {
        long n = 0;
        for (int i = 0; i < 200_000_000; i++) {
            byte[] b = new byte[64];   // dies immediately
            n += b.length;
        }
        System.out.println(n);
    }
}
```

```bash
java -Xmx256m -Xlog:gc Churn
```

You allocated ~12 GB through a 256 MB heap. Count the collections and add up the
pause times. Then change `byte[64]` to `byte[640]` and re-run — ten times the
allocation, and GC time barely moves, because the survivor count is still zero.

### Lab 2 — now make them survive

```java
public class Survive {
    static final List<byte[]> KEEP = new ArrayList<>();
    public static void main(String[] a) {
        for (int i = 0; i < 2_000_000; i++) {
            byte[] b = new byte[64];
            if (i % 4 == 0) KEEP.add(b);      // 25% survive
        }
        System.out.println(KEEP.size());
    }
}
```

Same allocation volume as a portion of Lab 1, far more GC time. **Run both and
compare — that contrast is the entire page.**

### Lab 3 — watch promotion in the log

```bash
java -Xmx512m -Xlog:gc*,gc+heap=debug,gc+age=trace -jar yourapp.jar
```

Find the age histogram lines. Watch objects age from 1 to the tenuring
threshold. Then shrink the survivor space:

```bash
java -Xmx512m -XX:+UseParallelGC -XX:SurvivorRatio=64 -Xlog:gc*,gc+age=trace ...
```

Observe objects promoted at age 1 — premature promotion, reproduced
deliberately.

### Lab 4 — measure your real allocation rate

On a running service:

```bash
jcmd <pid> JFR.start duration=60s filename=alloc.jfr settings=profile
```

Open in JDK Mission Control and look at TLAB allocation by call site. Compare
allocation rate against promotion rate. On most services the top allocator is a
surprise — logging, serialisation, or a stream pipeline nobody suspected.

### Lab 5 — see escape analysis delete an allocation

```java
static double dist(double x1, double y1, double x2, double y2) {
    Point d = new Point(x1 - x2, y1 - y2);
    return Math.sqrt(d.x * d.x + d.y * d.y);
}
```

Benchmark with JMH and `-prof gc`. Look at `gc.alloc.rate.norm` — bytes
allocated per operation. Then re-run with `-XX:-DoEscapeAnalysis` and compare.
Watching that number go from 0 to 16 B/op is the clearest possible proof.

**If you do one thing from this page, run Labs 1 and 2 back to back.** Same
allocation volume, wildly different GC cost, and the difference is one
`if` statement.

## 8. Leading on this

**Reframe the team's GC conversations.** When someone says "we have a GC
problem", the useful next question is "is it allocation rate or promotion
rate?" Those have completely different fixes, and conflating them sends people
optimising away short-lived objects while a cache with no eviction quietly
grows. GC is a symptom reporter, not usually the disease.

**Ban object pooling without a benchmark.** It is the most common
performance-motivated change that makes things worse: you replace a ten-
instruction pointer bump with a concurrent data structure, and you move objects
into the old generation. Pooling is right for genuinely expensive resources —
connections, threads, direct buffers — and wrong for ordinary objects.

**Make GC logging non-negotiable and always on.** It's nearly free and it's the
difference between diagnosing a production incident from evidence and guessing.
Same argument as heap dumps on OOM: you cannot add it after the fact.

**Resist flag-tuning as a first response.** The instinct to copy a
twenty-flag configuration from a blog post is strong and almost always wrong —
`-Xmn` fighting G1's adaptive sizing is the classic example. Change one thing,
measure, keep or revert.

**Teach the hypothesis, not the flags.** An engineer who understands that cost
scales with survivors will make better decisions about caching, batch sizes and
object lifetimes than one who has memorised `MaxGCPauseMillis`. The flags change
between JDK versions; the hypothesis doesn't.

## 9. Where to go deeper

- Jones, Hosking & Moss, *The Garbage Collection Handbook* — the reference.
  Chapters on copying collection and generational hypothesis are the relevant
  ones.
- Ungar (1984), "Generation Scavenging" — the original paper. Short, readable,
  and it's satisfying to see the hypothesis stated before it became folklore.
- JEP 439 (Generational ZGC) — the motivation section is an excellent modern
  restatement of why generations pay off.
- Aleksey Shipilëv, "JVM Anatomy Quarks" on TLABs and allocation — precise and
  short.
- Oracle's unified logging (`-Xlog`) documentation, so you can construct the
  exact log you want rather than copying one.
